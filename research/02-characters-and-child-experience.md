## Characters and Instructors

The instructors are central to the product experience.

Each character should have a recognizable personality, communication style, and animated presence.

### Initial character concepts

**Pip the Fox — Numbers and Math**
- Curious, playful, adventurous.
- Loves counting, patterns, and solving problems.
- Uses animals, objects, and environments to introduce math concepts.
- Encourages children to discover answers themselves.

**Bo the Bear — Stories and Language**
- Calm, patient, encouraging.
- Helps with storytelling, vocabulary, and comprehension.
- Asks children to predict events and explain ideas.
- Creates a comfortable space for experimentation.

**Ollie the Owl — Science and Curiosity**
- Thoughtful, inquisitive, fascinated by the world.
- Explores animals, weather, nature, and simple scientific ideas.
- Encourages questions and observations.

**Ziggy the Frog — Shapes and Patterns**
- Energetic, silly, expressive.
- Uses movement, shapes, puzzles, and sequences.
- Makes problem-solving feel like a game.

Pip remains the initial math instructor. Bo, Ollie, and Ziggy are exploratory future concepts; the MVP does not require a four-character roster.

### Potential instructor differentiation

Characters could eventually differ in:
- Personality and humour.
- Speaking pace and energy.
- Teaching approaches.
- Learning subjects.
- Animated environments.
- Types of interactive challenges.

The home screen prioritizes subjects. Characters lead the lessons within a subject; choosing an instructor is not the primary navigation model.

## Core Child Experience

### Home screen and adult setup

The home screen is organized around **subjects**, starting with **Math**. An adult selects and starts the lesson, handles any device or microphone setup, and hands over to the child.

The adult can step away while the child learns. Routine play and level transitions should not depend on adult help.

### Voice-only play

The child is not expected to read. All lesson instructions, questions, choices, feedback, and transitions must be understandable through speech and the visual scene.

The child progresses from level to level using only their voice. Touch, dragging, typing, or reading labels are not required for the child to complete a lesson. Parent setup and settings can use conventional controls.

### Example adventure

**Pip's Butterfly Garden — Let's Count!** remains the initial adventure concept.

Pip introduces a simple problem aloud:

“Some butterflies have landed in my garden! Can you help me find out how many?”

The scene shows the butterflies, the child answers aloud, and the next challenge follows the authored learning rules.

### Interactive lesson loop

1. Pip introduces the goal and explains how to play aloud.
2. The scene presents a short math challenge.
3. Pip asks one clear question.
4. The child responds by voice.
5. Sprout interprets the response and records the evidence.
6. Pip offers feedback, a clarification, or an appropriate hint.
7. The application advances the level or challenge when its authored conditions are met.
8. The lesson ends with a spoken recap and a natural stopping point.

Each lesson has a defined structure. The working duration is **5–10 minutes per lesson/session**, not an established attention-span limit. The exact relationship between a lesson and its levels remains to be designed; see [Learning and Curriculum](03-learning-and-curriculum.md).

### Proposed interaction details to test

These are design implications of voice-only play, not a finalized command vocabulary:

- Understand natural requests for help, repetition, a pause, and stopping.
- Explain choices aloud without requiring the child to memorize a menu.
- Clarify unclear speech without treating a recognition failure as a wrong math answer.
- Provide a spoken recovery path when the child hesitates or the session is interrupted.
- Make talking and listening states visible through character animation.

The character's speech animation must synchronize with the audio the child hears.

### Personalization

Over time, Sprout could adapt:
- Difficulty.
- Pacing.
- Types of examples.
- Amount of guidance.
- Concepts requiring additional practice.
- Preferred characters or activities.

Adaptation should be based on observable evidence and explicitly designed rules—not unsupported claims that the model has established mastery.
